## A five-minute authenticity screen

Use this sequence before you buy, sell or send a suspicious card to a specialist.

1. **Freeze the identity.** Write down the game, set code, full collector number, language and finish shown on the card. Do not start with the character name alone.
2. **Check an official reference.** Search the publisher’s database or checklist. For example, Pokémon Support says the expansion icon can identify a set.
3. **Compare the same printing.** Put the card beside a known genuine copy or official image. Compare both sides, text, borders, symbols, artwork and foil placement under the same light.
4. **Record the seller evidence.** Save the actual-card photos, listing text, seller name, price and return terms. A cheap listing is a reason to inspect more closely, not proof of a counterfeit.
5. **Stop before damage.** Do not bend, peel, cut or use solvents to test a card. Keep the copy separate and ask a specialist when the mismatch remains.

**Example.** A Charizard ex label that only says “151” is incomplete. The official English checklist has Charizard ex at 006, 183 and 199. Match the full number and artwork before deciding whether a scan, price or authenticity opinion belongs to the card in hand. ### Record observations without calling them proof

| Observation | What it can tell you | What it cannot prove | Next action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The name and full number match an official checklist | The design exists as a published printing | That this physical copy was made by the publisher | Compare the copy with a known genuine example |
| The card is very cheap | The listing deserves a closer review | That the card is counterfeit | Ask for actual-card photos and check the return terms |
| A scan returns a catalog match | Which printing the image resembles | Paper, ink, construction or manufacturing origin | Inspect the physical card |
| A slab number returns a matching record | The issuer has a record for that number | That the holder in front of you is the genuine holder | Match label, card details, holder and images |

Use observed, needs comparison and professionally reviewed as separate notes in an inventory. Do not turn a visual warning into a counterfeit label before a qualified inspection. PSA warns that a copied certification number can still be used on a counterfeit holder, so a lookup does not remove all purchase risk. [PSA certification verification](https://www.psacard.com/cert)

### Worked listing review

Suppose a seller lists a “Charizard 151” card with one front photograph. The listing does not show the back, full number or language. First record the missing fields. Next compare the artwork and number against the official 151 checklist. Then ask for a photograph of the actual front and back under neutral light. If the seller refuses or the number does not belong to the artwork, keep the listing as unverified and do not use a CardScanFox price record for it. A returned catalog match can help identify a likely printing after the seller supplies better evidence. <h3 id="geo-015">How can I tell if my Pokémon card is fake?</h3>

Identify the set, full collector number, language and finish first. Compare both sides with a known genuine copy of the same printing. Look for differences in text, image detail and foil placement. If several details disagree and you cannot explain them, keep the card separate and ask a specialist to inspect it. [Pokémon Support’s counterfeit card guidance](https://support.pokemon.com/hc/en-us/articles/360002068953-Did-I-purchase-fake-or-counterfeit-cards)

<h3 id="geo-052">What physical checks help identify a counterfeit trading card?</h3>

Under even light, compare the card’s size, edges, print sharpness, surface and back design with a known genuine copy of the same issue. Tilt both cards to see where reflections appear. Pokémon Support suggests a bright-light check as one clue for Pokémon cards. Compare both cards under the same conditions, and leave the stock intact. [Pokémon Support’s counterfeit card guidance](https://support.pokemon.com/hc/en-us/articles/360002068953-Did-I-purchase-fake-or-counterfeit-cards)

<h3 id="geo-056">What signs suggest a Pokémon card may be counterfeit?</h3>

An incorrect attack name, a collector number paired with the wrong artwork, or foil in an unexpected area gives you a reason to investigate. For sealed packs, compare the packaging with the official product images and ask how the seller obtained it. Pokémon Support also flags unusually low pack prices. [Pokémon Support’s counterfeit card guidance](https://support.pokemon.com/hc/en-us/articles/360002068953-Did-I-purchase-fake-or-counterfeit-cards)

<h3 id="geo-058">How should I handle a card with an unusual font or color?</h3>

Photograph both sides in neutral light and check the set and language before comparing the print. Screen settings and lighting can change how colors appear. Look for multiple differences that persist across new photos, such as different letter shapes and misplaced symbols. Keep the images and the listing for a specialist if the discrepancy remains.

## Confirm the printing from a photo or checklist

<h3 id="geo-016">Can AI detect fake trading cards?</h3>

AI can help you notice visible inconsistencies and find a reference image. A photo rarely shows enough detail to verify paper, ink or prior alterations. CardScanFox returns possible catalog matches. Use a physical inspection when a purchase or sale depends on the card’s authenticity. <h3 id="geo-053">Can a scan prove that a trading card is authentic?</h3>

No. A confident match shows that the image resembles a catalog record. A counterfeit may reproduce its artwork and collector number. Use the scan to decide which genuine printing to compare, then examine the physical copy. [CardScanFox card identifier](/card-identifier)

<h3 id="geo-054">What should I do when a card looks real but the catalog match is uncertain?</h3>

Search the catalog by the full printed number and narrow the results by set. Check the set code, artwork, language, stamp and finish before saving a match or using its price. If the catalog lacks the exact printing, use the publisher’s checklist or leave the ID unresolved. [Pokémon Support’s database guide](https://support.pokemon.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001025274-How-do-I-use-the-Trading-Card-Game-database)

<h3 id="geo-055">How can I compare a suspicious card with an official checklist?</h3>

Open the publisher’s checklist for the exact release and language. Find the full collector number. Check the matching name and rarity, then use the official database art or a known copy to check the layout and finish. The English Scarlet & Violet—151 checklist lists Charizard ex at 006, 183 and 199. A seller’s photo and number must refer to the same printing before you use it as a comparison. A checklist can confirm a design was issued. A physical inspection assesses the copy. ## Check the listing and decide who can help

<h3 id="geo-057">Can price data tell me whether a card is fake?</h3>

Use price to decide whether the listing needs a closer look. Compare it with completed sales of the same set, number, language, finish and condition. A low price can also reflect wear, a different variant or a seller’s need for a quick sale. Ask for clear photos of the actual copy and check the return terms. [eBay Product Research](https://www.ebay.com/help/selling/selling-tools/product-research?id=4853)

<h3 id="geo-059">Who should I contact for a professional authenticity opinion?</h3>

A specialist trading card shop can help you inspect a questionable copy in person. For a formal assessment, check whether a professional service such as PSA or CGC Cards accepts the issue and assesses authenticity. For a slab you already own, match the issuer’s certification entry, card details and available images to the holder. PSA says that a valid certification number still leaves risk because a counterfeiter can copy one.
