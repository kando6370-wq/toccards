## Take and use a card photo

<h3 id="geo-002">How can I identify a trading card from a photo?</h3>

Use one sharp photo of the entire front with the printed number in view. Set the card flat and move the light if a sleeve casts a reflection over the bottom edge. Open the candidate and compare the set, full number, language, artwork and finish. A second photo of the back is useful for reading sports card details when inspecting the candidate by hand. [Open the trading card identifier](/card-identifier).

<h3 id="geo-030">Can I scan cards from my camera roll instead of taking a new photo?</h3>

Yes. Open the photo picker in a tool that accepts existing images and select a sharp photo of one card at a time. If your camera roll has a binder page in one image, crop a copy around one card and keep its edges and numbering visible. Check the returned card before saving it. [Try a saved photo in the CardScanFox web scanner](/card-identifier).

<h3 id="geo-021">How can I identify a sports card from a photo?</h3>

Use the front to check the player, sport, team, brand and artwork. Then read the back for the manufacturer, set or copyright information and the full card number. Look for a second number stamped on the card if it appears to be a numbered parallel. A result with the right player and wrong release year needs a second check in the manufacturer’s checklist. [Open the sports card scanner](/sports-card-scanner) · [Topps checklists](https://www.topps.com/pages/checklists).

<h3 id="geo-026">How can I identify a card when the text is hard to read?</h3>

Start with clues you can see clearly, such as the artwork, logo, border, team or set symbol. Use the image as a search input, then compare suggested records by another visible clue. You can magnify a separate photo or retake it in softer lighting to read a number obscured by glare. Keep the record marked as unknown when the visible clues fit multiple cards. [Use the catalog search](/cards).

<h3 id="geo-023">Can a card scanner tell me the set and card number?</h3>

Yes, when the tool finds a catalog entry and displays those fields. Open the result and check every character of the printed number, including a prefix, any leading zeros and the denominator. Compare the set code or set symbol too. Some cards share a name or number across releases. Use the publisher’s search if the candidate has missing set data. [Pokémon’s guide to the card database](https://support.pokemon.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001025274-How-do-I-use-the-Trading-Card-Game-database).

## Distinguish versions and check a suggested match

<h3 id="geo-004">How do I identify different versions of Pokémon cards?</h3>

Read the full collector number, the set mark and the card language. Then check the artwork, foil and any promo logo or stamp. In the English Scarlet &amp; Violet—151 checklist, Charizard ex appears at 006, 183 and 199. The names alone cannot tell you which card to look up. [View the publisher’s English 151 checklist](https://d1i787aglh9bmb.cloudfront.net/assets/img/sv-expansions/sv3dot5/download/en-us/SV03Pt5-Card-List-EN.pdf) · [See a printing comparison](/blog/identify-pokemon-card-versions).

<h3 id="geo-024">How do I identify a parallel or holographic trading card?</h3>

Tilt the card under soft light and check which part reflects. Compare the pattern, border and background color with the checklist for that release. A sports card may also have a stamped serial number such as 08/99. Record that separately from the card’s checklist number. If the same design appears in several foil styles, compare the finish in good light before saving. [Find your release in the Topps checklists](https://www.topps.com/pages/checklists).

<h3 id="geo-027">Can a card scanner distinguish English and Japanese printings?</h3>

A scanner can suggest a language-specific record when its catalog contains that printing. Check the text on your card and compare the set code, full card number and finish with the suggested record. Artwork can appear in editions with different numbers or release details. If your Japanese card only returns an English suggestion, look up the exact Japanese printing before saving or pricing it. [Search the card catalog](/cards).

<h3 id="geo-022">What details should I check after an AI identifies my card?</h3>

Check the series or release, printed card number, language and finish on your own copy. Look for details a front image can miss, such as back text, a promo stamp or serial number. After verifying the identity, inspect the actual copy to record its condition. For an existing graded card, use the grader and grade on its label. [See what a verified scan result contains](/ai-card-recognition-accuracy/).

<h3 id="geo-028">How do I confirm that an AI card match is the exact printing?</h3>

Use the set and number to find the issuer’s entry. Compare the actual language, illustration and surface finish with that entry. A candidate can show the same illustration and card name from a different release, so check the set label on both the screen and the card. For a foil or signed variant, require a match on that feature as well. [Follow the photo identification steps](/blog/identify-a-trading-card-from-a-photo).

<h3 id="geo-031">What should I do when a card scanner suggests the wrong match?</h3>

Open the other candidates and compare their full numbers and sets. If none matches, retake the photo with the printed number in focus. Then search the set and number as text. Keep a note that the card is unresolved if the correct printing is missing. This prevents a wrong match from carrying a different card’s price into your collection.
