## Use a measurable storage check

The National Archives gives general paper-storage reference points of below 75°F and relative humidity below 65%, while warning that very low humidity can make materials brittle. These are preservation guidance for paper and photographs, not a card-grading guarantee. Use a hygrometer to observe your own room and move the collection away from water risk, direct heat and strong swings.

| Check | Action | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Prefer a cool, stable indoor shelf | A room reading does not describe the inside of a sealed box |
| Relative humidity | Watch readings during wet and dry periods | Do not treat one reading as a year-round record |
| Enclosure | Use a fitted, stable sleeve, pocket or holder | The material and fit still need inspection |
| Light and water | Avoid direct sun, leaks, pipes and condensation | Storage cannot reverse existing damage |

A practical review is to record the room reading, inspect one binder page and one box, and photograph any curl, damp mark or cracked slab before moving the cards. ## Protect cards from handling and their surroundings

Storage starts with handling. Wash and dry your hands, keep food and drinks away, and prepare a clean surface before opening a binder or box. Do not force a card through a tight pocket or use a solvent to remove a mark. Record a suspected issue and ask a conservator or grading service when the card is valuable. Pair this check with the [collection inventory guide](/blog/trading-card-collection-questions) so the physical location, condition note and image date stay together.

When a card is being considered for grading, photograph the existing condition before changing its holder. The [grading guide](/blog/trading-card-grading-questions) explains why a sleeve, slab and service submission should be recorded as separate steps. This prevents a storage change from being confused with a grade prediction.

## A practical storage and inspection checklist

Use this checklist when you add a card to a box or binder, then repeat it during a seasonal review.

- **Location:** Choose a clean interior shelf off the floor. Keep the collection away from windows, radiators, vents, pipes, damp basements, hot attics and garages.
- **Enclosure:** Use a correctly sized sleeve, pocket or holder. Check that the card is not forced into a pocket and that a thick card does not bow the page. Avoid PVC for long-term storage.
- **Support:** Keep cards upright or flat with enough support that they do not lean, bend or shift. Leave room to remove a card without dragging its corners.
- **Environment:** Put a small hygrometer near the collection. Record the reading during a wet week and after a hot day. Move the box if the area has large swings, condensation or a leak risk.
- **Inventory:** Record the exact printing, language, condition or slab grade, and the box or binder location. Photograph the front and back for valuable cards.
- **Review:** Look for curl, whitening, surface transfer, damp marks, pests and cracked slabs. Fix the storage condition before cleaning a card.

**Example.** A binder page bows around a thick card and the card catches when removed. Move that card to a holder sized for its thickness, keep the page supported, and record the new location. The National Archives recommends stable enclosures, suitable sizing and storage away from damp basements, garages, hot attics and water sources. ### Match the holder to the job

| Card situation | Practical choice | Check before you close the box |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard raw card | Fitted sleeve and a supported box or binder pocket | The card slides in without catching and does not bow the page |
| Thick or unusual-size raw card | Holder made for its thickness and size | Corners and edges have clearance |
| Graded card | Original slab plus a fitted outer case or padded compartment | Slabs do not rub or press against one another |
| Card being photographed | Clean, flat surface and indirect light | The card is handled by its edges and returned to its holder |
| Bulk review box | Sleeved cards with dividers and an inventory label | Cards stay upright or flat and cannot slide into a bend |

National Archives guidance for paper and photographs recommends stable plastics such as polyester, polypropylene or polyethylene and warns against PVC for long-term storage. The guidance is for archival materials, so check the card-holder manufacturer’s specifications as well. [National Archives general guidance](https://www.archives.gov/preservation/holdings-maintenance/general-guidance.html)

### Record an inspection without handling the card twice

For each valuable card, keep one inventory entry with the exact printing, language, finish, raw condition or slab company and grade, holder type, storage location, front and back image date, and the last inspection date. If you see curl, a cracked holder, damp marks or surface transfer, photograph the issue before moving the card. Move the storage container first, then decide whether a conservator or grading company should inspect the card. Do not clean a suspected surface mark with water or solvent. The Library of Congress warns that poor enclosures and unsuitable materials can damage photographic and paper objects over time. [Library of Congress handling guidance](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/photolea.html)

<h3 id="geo-096">How should I store trading cards long term?</h3>

Use clean, fitted sleeves and a well-supported box or binder. Choose an indoor shelf off the floor, away from pipes, vents and direct sunlight. Keep the cards supported so they cannot lean hard or slide. For example, inspect one box after a humid season. Check the lid and inside surfaces for damp marks, and look for cards that have curled or shifted. [Canadian Conservation Institute paper care](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/paper-objects.html)

<h3 id="geo-097">What sleeves and binders help protect cards?</h3>

Pick sleeves and binder pockets that fit the full card, with room for its thickness. Look for a manufacturer that identifies the material, such as non-PVC polypropylene. A thicker memorabilia card may need a dedicated holder. Slip a low-value card of the same dimensions into a sample pocket first. If the corners catch or the page bows, choose a larger pocket or a holder sized for the thicker card. [Ultra PRO binder guidance](https://support.ultrapro.com/en-US/articles/pages-binders-106968) · [Canadian Conservation Institute enclosure guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/conservation-preservation-publications/canadian-conservation-institute-notes/protective-enclosures-books-paper.html)

<h3 id="geo-098">How do humidity and heat affect trading cards?</h3>

High humidity can encourage mold. Repeated swings can make paper expand and shrink, which can cause distortion. Heat can speed up paper deterioration, and direct light adds fading risk. Place a hygrometer near your collection and record readings through a wet week. If conditions swing sharply beside a window or exterior wall, move the cards to a more stable indoor shelf. Preservation guidance for paper gives context, and different card coatings and holders can respond differently. [Canadian Conservation Institute storage guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/conservation-preservation-publications/canadian-conservation-institute-notes/storing-works-paper.html)

<h3 id="geo-099">What is the safest way to store valuable graded cards?</h3>

Keep the grading holder intact and use a fitted case or padded cabinet so slabs stay separated and protected against knocks. Store the case in a stable, secure location away from water and sunlight. Keep a separate record of each label and photographs of both sides. For a collection that needs professional storage, check service eligibility, policy coverage and retrieval terms. PSA Vault lists eligible grading companies and publishes its service terms. [PSA Vault](https://www.psacard.com/info/psa-vault) · [PSA Vault terms](https://www.psacard.com/vaultterms) · [Graded card tracking guide](/graded-card-tracker)

<h3 id="geo-100">How can I photograph cards without damaging them?</h3>

Set a camera above a clean, dry, flat surface and use even, indirect light. Handle a raw card by its edges with clean, dry hands. Keep an existing slab or protective sleeve in place when the label and edges are readable. If the photo catches glare, move the light or camera before handling the card again. Review the front and back images for visible corners and legible set numbers before you put the card away.
